Season 1 Episode 1 · February 20, 2026

Roman Pikulik: “I want the war in my family to end with me”

Hosts: Oleksandr Kyliushyk, Nadiia Kalachova

Roman is a husband, a father, a Christian and a veteran. He fought in the infantry and, after being wounded, served in administrative roles. A conversation about faith at war, returning to church and civilian life, honesty about mobilization, and the God who is faithful even when He doesn't answer our prayers.

The conversation is in Ukrainian. Below is the full English text.

Why have conversations like this?

Nadia: Roma, when we were getting to know each other, I looked at your Instagram and I really liked it. I even took a screenshot so people would remember not all the complicated things Sasha said, but something a bit simpler. Your Instagram says: “Husband, first of all a father, Christian and veteran.” I think that's exactly how people will remember you.

Our mutual friend Yura was honestly surprised when he found out you were coming to talk with us. He said you're not a public person. So tell me, please: despite not being a public person, what value do you see in conversations like today's? And is there really a demand for honest conversations without armor — about people, war and faith?

Roman: I'll try to answer in order. I think there's definitely a demand. I'd even say we need it. I personally need it: just to tell my story, to pour out my heart, to shape my own thoughts and my own perspective. I think a lot of veterans are ready to talk honestly. There's a whole cluster of non-public people — I'll always root for the infantry — who have their own special experience and have something to say to society. Maybe even subconsciously they're looking for a chance to talk about themselves, their experience, other people.

And to sum up what Sasha said: I was simply lucky to survive. That's how circumstances worked out. I'm grateful to my leaders and commanders who at some point kept me safe, didn't let me go, knocked some sense into me and stopped me. That's why my wife has a husband and my kids have a father.

Oleksandr: I also agree that it's very important to talk about this. Because usually we distance ourselves from it: theology is only for church or seminary, and what's happening in the world only concerns the world. I'd like to show in practice how Christians and Christianity in general work in this world, how Christ is in the middle of all this and how He supports us. And, as Roma emphasized, partly thanks to this we […]

Experience of service

Nadia: […] those who've had more of the grief, more of this war. So it's more because of your military experience?

Roman: Yes. I don't want to call myself some kind of professional infantryman — I don't want to devalue the guys. If I position myself as a “good infantryman,” then what do I call them? I don't want to devalue their unique experience. Besides, those who served with me know: I was in the infantry, then I got wounded, ran around with the infantry a bit more, and after that I was mostly in administrative positions.

A Christian at war

Nadia: Okay. Question: how much did your Christianity — the fact that your Instagram says “Christian” and below that “veteran” — define your experience of service? Roma at war, Roma the Christian — what did that mean for you?

Roman: It definitely mattered. One of those moments — if we go back to 2014: why I went with a clear conscience. Because I wanted to serve those guys […]

People tell me: “You know, it's such a shame that they laugh like that.” But for me it's so nice, it's praise when others laugh at someone: “You ended up, so to speak, in church — with Pikulik.” That's the church analogy they drew. I think people saw it. And that's how I positioned myself: I'm different, I'm not a career soldier, my approach is a bit different, and I'm in my place. I tried to serve people more and, where possible, to save them.

Nadia: Sasha, at some point everything has to go off plan, so fair warning: we're changing the order of questions now. Roma, Sasha mentioned the classic signs of a pre-war — or maybe not only pre-war — “proper” Christian: doesn't drink, doesn't smoke, doesn't swear. Can you stay a “sterile” Christian at war, one who doesn't drink, smoke or swear? Is that even realistic?

Roman: I think it's realistic. I didn't manage it, but I think it's realistic. For example, when the guys who […]

A commander's responsibility

Roman: […] then you can talk about changing position or direction of work. How many infantrymen we took… I think this is one of the important values, especially in an infantry battalion: you have to go through the infantry, and then you better understand the importance of everything else. Of course, an infantryman of 2022, 2023 and 2025 — those are three different kinds of service. But there are still basic principles.

So I wanted a person to take responsibility for where they were going. So that afterwards their families — wives, parents — wouldn't tell me: “Why didn't you take care of him, why didn't you keep my husband or son safe?” I didn't want to take that responsibility on myself.

Church, grace and expectations

Oleksandr: Following from that, another interesting question. Don't you feel the church is now more tolerant of people who pay bribes to dodge service than of those who fight and who might let some swearing slip?

Roman: The way I understand it: since I expect grace toward myself, I want to give grace to other people and to other churches. This war has, you could say, knocked a lot of people down. Wounds have opened, a lot of pus has come out. And I think that's good — these things need to be talked about. Was our society that sterile before the war? Definitely not. We just talked about it less.

Nadia: So you don't get the thought creeping in: “Everyone owes me, because I sacrificed my time, my family, all of this — so give me more grace”?

Roman: Maybe sometimes it creeps in. But it's definitely not constructive, it doesn't help me. What helps me more is taking responsibility for myself and my actions. Expecting more from someone won't help anyone. And in fact, the expectations I have of people or of those around me will never be met to the extent I expect. There's always a chance that something wasn't given to me, that the mission misunderstood something, didn't give enough grace. I'm more about something else: okay, I have my experience — how can I serve with it, what can I give?

Nadia: You don't burst into church shouting “I fought”?

Roman: “I fought, I burned in a tank” — no. I once said in the mission, when the mission director came out East (which I'm very grateful for): “I just want to come to some conference in a hoodie, sit down so no one bothers me, and just sit and listen.” That's the ideal moment of returning to the mission family for me. At the conference I'm a bit odd or shell-shocked, but I'm sitting there and not bothering anyone.

The last time, maybe three weeks ago, I was at my friend's church in Dubliany. I came in and sat in the back corner. They tell me: “Come to the front.” And I say: “No, this is the perfect spot — two walls behind my back.” I'd even hang a sign there for veterans: “Grannies, don't take it, this is our spot.” Grannies are my pain. But there were plenty of seats, so no granny suffered because of me.

The back pew isn't isolation

Oleksandr: See, the commander pushed a regular soldier out into the open and hid himself in the greenery.

Nadia: That wasn't Roma's idea, sorry, I'll defend Roman here. But I'll still dig deeper, because I don't fully understand what you're saying. Why hide? Why the back pew? Maybe there are so many misunderstandings that you don't even want to enter that dialogue? Or do you just not have the resources and strength for it right now? What does your “I'll be in a hoodie at the conference,” “in the back pew” mean?

As someone who hasn't fought and who'd like us to simply be good communities, I'm curious: say I see a guy sitting in a hoodie, and I know it's Roman, who fought. As a Christian who wants to be kind, what should I do — leave you alone or talk to you? What does this position of yours mean for me and for everyone?

Roman: Good question. I think the position means: “Give me a little more time. Don't push. Let me adapt here, come into harmony with myself, understand who I am, who my kids are, whom I haven't seen for several years.” So my position about the back pew isn't about isolation. It's more about personal space and about time.

I'm ready to talk more about these topics. Just let there be an adequate, good, safe space where we can talk about them. If we're going to talk about clouds, the weather, kittens, cars — we might as well not talk. But if we're going to talk about the war — let's talk deeply, not superficially.

Nadia: Sasha, can I ask a follow-up? We'll avoid the kittens. What does talking about the war differently mean for you? Because we want to make a whole podcast here about people, faith and war.

Roman: Not superficially. Not like: “We've got a 20-minute lunch, we need to fill the airtime, let's quickly talk about some light questions” — and everyone runs off. And space… It's not that I want to raise my own value. Let's take not Roman but any person who is a church member or not, but has some community. My idea is: “Friend, you have a unique experience, you're valuable to us, we want to hear you more. Let us do an interview or a meeting where you'll be comfortable and we can ask questions.”

Usually people have a lot of questions too, even ones like: “Is it okay to come over and say hi?” It's appropriate to ask: “How are you feeling?” And for the person that's valuable — that their experience is valued and that people want to hear them.

So my position about the back pew isn't about isolation. It's more about personal space and about time.

Roman Pikulik

Strangers after the war

Roman: […] the path of service — those I trust and I'm ready to let them into my life more. But the people we didn't even text with in these four years… I won't say I'm ready to hug them right away, because we're strangers.

I became a stranger to my wife. Three years at war — I came back a different person, and my wife became a different person. Never mind someone I just used to see around. Expecting that I came back the same and that we'll immediately restore a January-2022-style relationship that hasn't existed for three or four years — that's hard for me.

That's why it's important to support a veteran and his family while he's still serving. To be with that person in different positions, even just to ask. Some people say: “I don't write because I think you're on a mission and my call will get in the way.” Nobody works like that. If I'm on a mission […]

Triggers in civilian life

Oleksandr: […] the actual return to civilian life. Are there things that annoy you most in civilian life right now? Let me throw some triggers at you: “the prayer front,” “the economic front,” “the cultural front.” Or when people come up to you: “We prayed for you — we didn't write, but we prayed.”

Nadia: Can I clarify: was that your list of triggers?

Oleksandr: For me, “the prayer front” is a big one. There are moments when people explain that it's on the same level of importance as you fighting: “It's just as hard for us.”

Nadia: Maybe another list of triggers? I don't know it — it's your list, to annoy him more. I'm the one doing the triggering here.

Oleksandr: To sum up, the question was: is there anything that annoys you now?

Roman: Everything! But to be precise — what I miss… I can't drive the way I drove in the army. I miss those emotions, no traffic lights, those rear-wheel-drive pickups. I don't have a weapon always within reach: whether you use it for its purpose or not, whether you play with it in the evenings — but it's there. I miss that too.

There are triggers, of course. But maybe this is my experience: I try to be grateful even to those people. Obviously I have my own opinion on certain things. But I'm grateful, because there are people who just didn't care at all: didn't pray, didn't help in any way. And I can't say all of that is worth nothing. There are many people I've never seen: we meet, and that person has been praying for you and your family for years. I'm grateful, because that was their contribution. Especially when people overseas, on the other side of the world, objectively could have not lifted a finger and not worried about Ukraine, about this whole war.

If we didn't have this community… I just feel sorry for people who are afraid to entrust their lives and the lives of their children and wives to God, who can take care of them — including through community.

And the question that triggers me very often is this. Why do hundreds, if not thousands, of non-believers who don't have a church community and all these opportunities go and are ready to lay down their lives — we can talk about motives separately sometime — why do they go into the Defense Forces and the Armed Forces of Ukraine? While believers, who usually have community, good health, jobs, strong family relationships, don't go to serve. Even though in fact the one who goes has nothing and loses everything. And we declare that we have eternal life, that our home is in heaven — lots and lots of right words. And here we have a pretty good life. And we want someone else to protect us and someone else to pay that price.

Oleksandr: But you said: our home is in heaven. So what's the point of defending it here?

Roman: All the more so — if your home is in heaven, take the express elevator to heaven, and everyone wins. If you say this is a temporary shelter and a temporary life — why fear it, if you trust? At some point I declared this to myself. I understand that God can take care of things better: He definitely won't abandon my kids and my wife, He'll take care of them better than I would, because I'm sinful, lazy, with my own issues. But God is faithful. So what should I fear? I want my kids to grow up with a father and my wife with a husband. But this gave me freedom.

Of course, later my son once told me: “I thought you'd died.”

Nadia: How old is your son?

Roman: He'll be five in March. When I left for the war in 2022, he was 11 months old […]

Does the war pull you back?

Roman: […] Does the war pull me back? The guys definitely do. I'm sick of a certain Soviet-style system, of reports — I hate all of that. But I definitely miss the guys, the adrenaline, the smell of gun oil — all of that. Right now I see it as just a pause from the army. And later I'll need to go back in.

And I'd like the war in my family to end with me. I don't want my kids to have that experience. That's one of the important reasons for me too.

I admire older men. In my service there were several moments when a man is serving and he already has a son of college age. And he says: “Volodymyrovych, if my son goes into the army, I'm the first to go to the infantry position. I don't want him to serve. I'll take it on myself, I'll stay here — so my son has a ticket to life.” To me that's a very beautiful example of a father's love. Maybe my father raised me that way — that children should bury their parents, not parents their children. I can't even imagine the pain parents go through when they bury their children.

So that's one of the moments when I think about the war. Of course, also to close some of my inner gestalts, unfinished business in the army. But I don't want my kids, my boys, to serve in the army. Let alone my daughter.

Nadia: Do you believe these questions can be closed? Is there a line where Roman will say: “That's it, I'm done”?

Roman: Objectively — I probably will never close them. But you can get closer. I talked with my friend, a psychiatrist and psychotherapist. He says: “Roma, I've had clients who said the same thing. They had the experience you dream of, and they still think they haven't had enough grief, they want to try again.” And there is that logic. To put it the way the guys would — I haven't had my fill of fighting. Some say: “You're jammed” or “you've been soldered shut.”

This state suits me, and I don't want anything re-soldered in there. For me it's a normal state. Some call it my bubble. For me it's not a bubble — it's my real life. I won't say I'm always comfortable in it, but I don't want to leave it. For me, being a civilian is definitely death.

Oleksandr: I agree. People asked me too: when will this end? And would it have been enough if I'd died in the war? I'd have thought I died too early or didn't fight enough.

Roman: There's a phrase: only the dead have seen the end of war. It's interpreted differently, but there's logic in it. If you've been through war, even when the fighting ends, the war inside you won't end. You have to learn to live with it.

Nadia: So now you've moved into a new stage of an inner war?

Roman: A new stage, I'd say… If we go by the stages as they were counted — the fourth stage and preparing for the fifth.

Nadia: You started talking about your own stuff, and I don't know what's appropriate to ask you in this context.

Roman: You can ask anything.

Nadia: Then let's talk about God. When I was getting to know Sasha, I did a trial interview with him without cameras. It was great, because without cameras you can ask a lot of silly questions and not think about wording. I asked, for example: “Sasha, what do you think now about the power of prayer?” He asked me to repeat it several times. I wanted to know how he understands it for himself now and whether he believes in the power of prayer. So let's talk about your experience of God.

And I'd like the war in my family to end with me.

Roman Pikulik

The God who is faithful

Roman: […] During the war I understood: God is sovereign. I have no complaints or questions for Him about why You allowed this or why You let this person die. I simply understood that God is sovereign and faithful.

If there were questions I asked God, they were these: why are You so patient with me? Why haven't You killed me yet, punished me? Why are my kids still alive? How much more do You have… How can there be so much mercy both toward me and toward other people?

What does it mean that God is faithful and merciful? For me He's faithful in that even when I'm unfaithful, He does His part. And it doesn't depend on me. God's love, His sovereignty, don't depend on my actions or on anyone's mood.

And God is also faithful in that He didn't answer my prayers when I asked Him to just take me: “God, I can't handle it anymore. This would probably be a good moment. The kids will remember their father — or they won't. There'll be some decent photos left, their father will be a hero.” That's God's faithfulness — that He didn't answer my requests to go to Him sooner. Because for me that would have been the easier path — to get rid of everything and hand the responsibility to my deputy: let someone else deal with it.

Nadia: “There'll be decent photos”… I thought there'd be something serious and theological now… sorry.

Roman: Well, what — decent photos. The best, top photos were lost on a flash drive in Popasna.

Nadia: I won't let you hide behind humor. I remember from our conversation that this is your defense mechanism. You said similar things in your interview with Andrii Hutsuliak: you were pulling out a wounded man, and you also thought: “Oh, a good moment to die heroically and nobly — and they'll even carry out my body.”

Honesty instead of loopholes

Nadia: Okay, Roma. Has anything peeled away from your Christian life? Some practices that turned out not to work, to be lifeless, and you set them aside?

Roman: I always push for honesty — even in motives. Calling things by their names. This is my trigger, why I sometimes grumble in conversations with Sasha and other veterans: why can't Christians honestly say “I don't want to go into the army, I'm afraid”? Instead they start twisting, looking for loopholes, putting on badges and patches, taking up some ministry. A good ministry, a beautiful one — that's all fine. But with the goal of not getting drafted.

I'm fine if someone honestly says: “I do this ministry because it gives me certain privileges: the enlistment officer will let me go or say — good for you, you're doing such important work, keep it up.” And it is important work. But I respect those who are honest. It meant a lot to me when someone says: “I'm afraid. I really don't want to leave my wife, my family. I'm afraid that in the army they'll take away my freedom.” I was glad to see in western Ukraine that there are people who can say that honestly. And there are people who maneuver.

I probably dodge in many moments myself: covering some of my motives with good values and ministry. But I'm for honesty, especially in questions of mobilization and military service. People think they'll lose their freedom. And I say the opposite: a person gains freedom by going into the army — a clear conscience before themselves, their future children, their country. I feel very sorry for a man who lives in fear for three or four years. So you can grab freedom. You can grab freedom altogether and go straight to God. Or you can serve here a little longer.

Oleksandr: I think the issue here is that not everyone wants to hear and discuss this. I really hope that thanks to this podcast we'll preserve these conversations. And those for whom this becomes relevant will go up to veterans and soldiers, talk with them and ask questions. As Roman said: it takes time, and then he's open to talk. I think that's exactly why we're doing this. I'd like there to be a desire to hear, to talk and to change something.

Lightning round

Nadia: Lightning round! Sasha, do you want to do it yourself or take turns?

Oleksandr: I can do it.

Roman: Are you expecting detailed answers from me? Or “yes,” “no,” “I don't know”?

Oleksandr: “Yes” or “no” and briefly. If you want, you don't have to elaborate. First question: is death scary or just a transition?

Roman: “Yes” or “no” doesn't work here. It's scary. I think it's a mercy that you still have to earn well.

Oleksandr: What's harder: surviving shelling or explaining to your kids why dad is leaving again?

Roman: My kids didn't understand. When I left, they were fine — they were younger. So I don't explain it to them. They don't know, they haven't had a taste of war. And that's good.

Oleksandr: What do you miss most from your wartime?

Roman: The guys, the team, the weapons, the fuel, the action in the pickups.

Oleksandr: The biggest civilian myth about soldiers?

Roman: That everyone dies right away and everyone ends up in the infantry.

Oleksandr: Did you cry at the front?

Roman: Yes. And I cried today too.

Oleksandr: Which book of the Bible became closest to you during the war?

Roman: Proverbs. You could quickly grab what you needed there. Read a little — and it got through to you, about life. You didn't have to go on and on about historical context. All of that matters, but there…

Oleksandr: Is it easier at war to be a believer or a non-believer?

Roman: I think it's always easier to be a believer. You have the Holy Spirit working with you. In most cases you're in a winning position: even if something happens and you die — you still win. And if you survive — you're with God.

Oleksandr: Who is God to you today: a judge, a friend, a commander or someone else?

Roman: I want Him to be like the Father to whom the prodigal son returns.

Oleksandr: We started with the question of whether this conversation makes sense. Did it?

Roman: Definitely. I'm very glad I had this opportunity. Thank you all — both the team and the listeners. This was important to me.

Nadia: Maybe some wise words to finish?

Roman: I can't remember any, I'm just a regular grunt. I wanted to say something reflective, but I can't manage wise talk right now. I'm proud that I don't know many wise words. That approach suits me, because in the unit everything is simple: “Go, do it, and don't show off.”

Nadia: Thank you so much, friends!
