Season 1 Episode 5 · July 17, 2026

Diana Havryliuk: “The war taught me that God is in everything and everywhere”

Hosts: Oleksandr Kyliushyk, Nadiia Kalachova

Diana is a volunteer with the “Hospitallers” medical battalion and a student in the Christian Counseling program at the Ukrainian Baptist Theological Seminary (UBTS). She grew up in a pastor's family. A conversation about the path from volunteering abroad to evacuating the wounded, why there's nothing heroic about it, vulnerability, unfiltered prayer, and the God who is present in everything.

The conversation is in Ukrainian. Below is the full English text.

Serving in a volunteer battalion means you can stop at any moment. And absolutely every rotation is my own free decision… For me to do something heroic, I'd pretty much have to lose my mind. Everything I'm doing now is within the bounds of rationality, common sense and my calling. So I don't see anything heroic in it.

Diana Havryliuk

Introduction

Nadia: This podcast is made in partnership between the Ukrainian Baptist Theological Seminary and the mission Ukraine for Christ. And today we're glad to welcome our guest Diana Havryliuk. Diana is a volunteer with the “Hospitallers” battalion and — which we're especially pleased about — a UBTS student in the Christian Counseling program. Diana, we're glad you're with us today.

Diana: Likewise.

Nadia: I just introduced you. When we look at you, a young woman, and hear: “Volunteer, Hospitallers medical battalion, evacuates the wounded, provides first aid,” an image immediately forms of a decisive, brave young woman. But I want to start from further back. Tell us, what shaped you before the moment we met? What made you who you are?

Family and values

Diana: People. One hundred percent people. First of all the family I was born into. My parents contributed a great deal to my formation. I was very lucky: my parents are very high-quality people, if you can put it that way. Very dedicated: first to God, second to each other, third to their ministry. And accordingly — to me and my younger sister. When people are well-formed themselves and have clear values, they pass those values on to their children. And when the children grow up, as in my case, they consciously decide to accept those values for their own lives and follow them. So my parents, friends, my close circle, the seminary, my classmates, church — any environment where I'm among people shapes me. Probably God works in me most through people.

Oleksandr: It's interesting that a young woman who grew up in a pastor's family decided to join the Hospitallers. At what moment did you realize you couldn't stay on the sidelines? Because it's not a very typical picture.

Diana: Hard to say. To me it actually seems very typical, because I grew up in a certain atmosphere. For me it's logical: my whole life I watched my parents, trusting God, do what He told them. And I saw that despite difficulties and trials it brings fulfillment and joy in life. So when you find yourself in circumstances like war, you understand that you definitely can't stay on the sidelines. If a child grows up in a family of deeply dedicated Christians, the result is her conscious and brave decisions when she grows up.

Oleksandr: But you could have served in church, kept helping displaced people and so on.

Diana: Why didn't you say that earlier? I could have, I could have! But here my own life philosophy comes into play: based on your context and the resources you have, you decide where it's most rational for you to be right now. I assessed my life situation and understood that at this stage it's logical to be in the battalion. Although it was a very gradual path: from volunteering for Ukraine abroad to working directly with soldiers. I guess I climbed all the steps little by little.

Step by step

Nadia: Tell us, please, about these stages. How old were you when the full-scale war began? Twenty. You're volunteering abroad — how does the idea of becoming a volunteer medic come about?

Diana: The full-scale war found me studying abroad, two months before finishing university. The first reaction, like most people's, was to drop everything and go to Ukraine. But my parents convinced me to finish the two months and get my diploma. While I was studying, the students and I organized a hub where aid for Ukrainians was brought in.

In May I came home and joined the volunteer center at our church: we handed out food kits, cooked lunches, held fellowship gatherings for displaced people. Then I started going on evacuations of civilians from frontline areas — we worked in the Bakhmut district during heavy fighting. Then I went many times on rotations as an assistant with a team of dentists — my uncle is a dentist. And when the project expanded and my role there shrank, the next logical step was to take on more responsibility. The Hospitallers became a very logical decision. Every step is a higher level of responsibility.

Oleksandr: At 20 I was going to the seaside and eating shawarma… I'd have done the same if it weren't for the war! See, I've already justified myself in my head. But look: between the decision to join the Hospitallers and actually becoming a Hospitaller, a year passes — because your parents were against it. What did your discussions look like and how did you convince them?

Diana: I didn't convince them — that's not my business; they had to settle this question with God. The first conversations were quite conflictual. But at some point I found a short course in Kyiv, signed up, and simply presented my parents with the fact that I was going to training. And my dad says: “Let me pay for it.” And I understood that they were moving to the stage of acceptance and trust.

The first rotation

Nadia: What did you imagine the front line would be like, and what did the reality turn out to be?

Diana: Since before that I'd been going with the mobile dentistry team very close to the front line and had been there during strikes, it wasn't something radically new for me.

Nadia: Still, the first rotation is always memorable. Was it special for you?

Diana: I remember a case from my first rotation. A wounded man, his whole body bandaged, burns from head to toe, his hair burned off, he barely spoke. I needed to photograph his Form 100 on his own phone so the document wouldn't get lost. I take the phone out of his zip bag — it's covered in dirt, and the lock screen photo shows him, his wife next to him, a little son in his arms and a little daughter on his lap. I look at that photo — and there's a lump in my throat and a knot in my stomach. Because I understand that these people most likely don't yet know what condition their husband and father is in. In that moment it opened up to me: this isn't just a wounded patient, it's a person who has a family, connections, feelings. The hardest thing is when a patient becomes human to you like that.

Right then I got a message from my uncle: “Diana, how are you?” It was so timely and important. I understood how important it is to reach out to people — family, friends.

Another big contributor to my emotional stability is the habit of writing notes after evacuations. Written reflection helps me lay out my thoughts in a structured form, lift the weight off my soul, and turn it into new experience or a small piece of my own worldview.

How to recover

Nadia: Are there things you've seen that still won't let go of you emotionally?

Diana: No. I think for now my ways of recovering work quite well: my reflections, my family — dad, mom, sister, my safe circle where I'm as relaxed as possible. And friends with well-developed emotional intelligence who come over after rotations and bring me back to normal life.

Nadia: And do you allow yourself to cry?

Diana: It's hard for me. I respect people's ability to cry and understand how important that function is for physical and psychological health. But I myself am a hostage to certain stereotypes about myself.

Nadia: Your goal is to stay vulnerable. Is that realistic?

Diana: To stay vulnerable, you first have to become vulnerable. As C. S. Lewis said, to love at all is to be vulnerable. And I love this life very much. Vulnerability is an expression of love for people and for life in general. Through vulnerability you confirm the sincerity of your love.

Through vulnerability you confirm the sincerity of your love.

Diana Havryliuk

Ordinary people, not titans

Oleksandr: What kind of people have you seen at war?

Diana: Everyone I've seen are very ordinary, simple people. They had their own lives, goals, plans, which they put on pause — by their own will or not — and now they're trying to win the right to go on living.

Oleksandr: Do you see a tendency to mythologize soldiers as “titans” or, on the contrary, to devalue them?

Diana: In 2022–2023 soldiers were portrayed as superheroes and titans, and that created distance between them and society. Now another, even worse tendency has appeared — devaluation, when some start to see service as a “wasted life.” This is a consequence of false mythologizing from the very beginning — instead of seeing in a soldier an ordinary person with their own pain and weaknesses.

Unfiltered prayer

Nadia: What does your prayer look like at home, and how does it sound on rotations?

Diana: Before the battalion I tended to season my prayers somewhat with beautiful or literary words. Now I allow myself much more completely unfiltered prayers: expressing everything that's on my heart and not being afraid to tell God everything directly.

The war taught me to be more aware of God's omnipresence and His greatness. If before I looked for that greatness in church architecture or in music, at war I see that God is present in absolutely all processes: next to me, with the wounded who are making their way out of positions, and even in global events.

Life, death and calling

Oleksandr: How has your attitude toward life and death changed?

Diana: I've come to love life even more. And death has become closer and more real. Although on my evacuations, thank God, not a single patient has died, the loss of loved ones taught me that every death is hard. Psychologically the hardest thing to accept is death when it's the result of another person's deliberate decision to take a life.

Nadia: How would you describe your calling yourself?

Diana: I use my gifts where they're most effective right now. I love to learn, so medical knowledge comes easily to me. And I'm good at listening: during evacuations I actively listen to the wounded, and that helps them release their pain.

Oleksandr: How do patriotism and Christianity fit together in you?

Diana: Very simply. Being a citizen of a country means there are other people around you who are also citizens. And who are they? Neighbors. And what does the Bible say to do with your neighbor? Love and serve. That's exactly what I'm doing — I'm simply living by the Bible.

Three pieces of advice for Ukrainian Christians

Nadia: What three key things would you like to say to Ukrainian Christians?

Diana:

  1. The vertical. Keep your vertical relationship with God well-tuned. Root your identity and worldview in Him, develop your relationship with Him.
  2. The horizontal. Invest in relationships with the people around you. Find a soldier, man or woman, support them, call them, be a place of safety and recovery for them.
  3. Read and be filled. Read a lot of the Bible and good books — C. S. Lewis, Remarque, Stus, Symonenko, Tim Keller. In critical moments quotes and truths fly into your head and give you strength to stand firm. For me, one such phrase was a line by Vasyl Stus about enduring — that patience is what polishes you.
