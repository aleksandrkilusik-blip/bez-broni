Season 1 Episode 7 · October 16, 2026

Artur “Sonic” Safonov: “The war will never end for me”

Hosts: Oleksandr Kyliushyk, Nadiia Kalachova

Artur is a volunteer, former commander of an assault squad in the Ukrainian Volunteer Corps “Right Sector,” a children's football coach and father of three. Before the full-scale invasion he worked in the sports ministry of the mission Ukraine for Christ. A conversation about the volunteer's path, brotherhood, losing brothers-in-arms, disappointment in people but not in God, and why the war will never end for him.

The conversation is in Ukrainian. Below is the full English text.

Where the call sign “Sonic” came from

Oleksandr: You're listening to the podcast “Without Armor.” Here we talk about people, faith and war — very openly. I'm really glad to welcome our guest Artur Safonov. Before the full-scale invasion Artur worked as a children's coach and was involved in the sports ministry of the mission Ukraine for Christ. At 27 he went to war as a volunteer and served as a squad commander in an assault unit. And after the birth of his third child he returned to civilian life. Artur, glad to welcome you to our podcast!

Artur: Hi! Thanks for the invitation.

Oleksandr: I'll start right away with something interesting. Your call sign is “Sonic.” Why that one? Maybe not everyone has seen the cartoon and the movie — Nadia was just asking before this: “What's that movie?”

Artur: Honestly, my wife suggested the call sign, and my son confirmed it. Before I left for the service, we watched the movie “Sonic.” I really liked the character himself: his wildness, the urge to be on the move, friendship, energy. Then I got a haircut, I had this short buzz. My son rubs my head and says: “You're like Sonic, really spiky!” That's where it came from.

And I finally confirmed the call sign at the training camp when I set a record on the obstacle course. We even had a joke that we were a special unit of “Sonics.” If we were heading out somewhere for a job, right away: “Okay, Sonics, let's go!”

A volunteer's path

Oleksandr: When you went to war, you were 27. And before that the enlistment office turned you down: “27 years old — okay, but you have no combat experience.” But you insisted. Why?

Artur: I couldn't wrap my head around it: how can it be — people are needed, but they don't take people? It started when I joined the Territorial Defense. First I was in the Kyiv one, then in Ternopil. In Ternopil I stood at a checkpoint and didn't understand what I was doing there with that little baton: letting cars through, checking that there were no dashcams… Then they lined us up and asked: “Who's ready to go to war?” I say: “I'm ready!” — “Good, we'll call you.” I wait a day, I wait a second day. I found their contacts, I call: “You were supposed to call me — did you forget?” And they say: “You don't have combat experience, you need to wait.”

And I started looking for other places where I could serve and be useful. Purely by chance I came across “Right Sector” — the Ukrainian Volunteer Corps, where I spent my entire service. I got into a three- or four-day training course with them and realized I liked it: the sense of brotherhood, serious training, a spiritual context — prayer in the morning. At the end of the training they ask: “Who's ready for war?” I say: “I'm ready!” — “Let's go tomorrow.” — “No, wait, I need to talk it over with my family.”

A decision made together with his wife

Oleksandr: Why was it so important for you specifically to go to war, to take the initiative, to say “I'm ready to go”? What was the main thing?

Artur: There were many things. First: before the full-scale invasion I was a football coach. And when I saw how children in Kyiv were hiding in basements as planes flew over and everything around was booming, how they cried and were afraid — I told myself I didn't want to see this. I had to influence it, even by one percent.

Second: my sister was under occupation in Kakhovka. For four or five days she was getting out of there under constant shelling. And that was exactly the time of Bucha, Irpin — people shot, children… As a man I felt responsible: okay, I can — so I have to go.

Oleksandr: At that time you had a wife and two children. How did she react? What did your conversations look like?

Artur: She said: “I could see you'd go to war anyway, because you couldn't find peace in the Territorial Defense.” I was constantly preparing, learning myself and teaching others as an instructor. I understood that a greater responsibility would fall on her. When I came back from training, I said straight out: “Lera, look: if we decide I'm going to war, the kids and all the household matters will be on you. And you need to be ready for the fact that I might die, and you'll be left alone with the kids and this responsibility. Are you ready?”

She answered: “I knew this conversation would come someday. But I ask you: settle this question with God, and I'll be ready to follow you, whatever the decision.” That was a very important moment for me. I consulted my brothers-in-arms — Roma Pikulik, Zhenia. They said: “You have to make this decision yourself and take responsibility for it yourself.” And I decided to go.

From instructor to assault squad commander

Oleksandr: And what were the arguments for going specifically into a combat unit? After all, you were offered a role as a physical training instructor preparing soldiers.

Artur: I felt I was doing too little and could do more. I wanted to get a taste of war. I was told there was an opportunity to go to a combat unit, go through a baptism of fire, smell the gunpowder and come back as an instructor with combat experience. We went through tough training and strict selection: out of 47 people, 11 were taken into the assault company. We felt like little T-rexes.

But when we got there, the guys we'd become friends with said: “Sonic, we're ready to trust you with our lives. We want you to stay with us as our commander.” That was a concrete decision. I called my commander at the training camp: “Sorry, I'll prepare a replacement, but I'm staying with the guys.” From that moment I became a squad commander in an assault unit.

Oleksandr: What did this responsibility mean for you — when the guys entrusted you with their lives?

Artur: I truly understood it when we were already in position and the fighting began. You have to lead the fight and be responsible for people. The first baptism of fire: a firefight starts, the guys empty almost everything from every barrel in 15 seconds. And you're in command: you pull one back by the collar, that one's wounded, first we repel the assault, then we give first aid… Managing that was hard.

But the hardest thing about command is being responsible for their emotional and psychological state. They dump on you everything that's gotten to them, and you soak it up like a sponge. And you can't show weakness, because it'll affect their morale. You just go into the woods, scream, pray, come back in a good mood and support the boys.

Young “Sonics” and brotherhood

Oleksandr: Tell us about your soldiers. What was the atmosphere like?

Artur: I had seven guys — from 18 to 27 years old, and one who was 34. Really young guys! What they feared most was that we'd go into combat and they wouldn't be taken, because they'd read somewhere that you can only go into combat from age 21. One got out of occupation in the Kherson region at 19 and went to serve. Another is my close brother Vasia. At 18 he ran away from his family in Germany: he told his mom he was going for a run, took his passport and came to fight in Ukraine. No one would take him, but he still found the Volunteer Corps.

The atmosphere was unreal. How we danced, how we sang Christmas carols! Water's dripping on your head, and you feel wonderful. That feeling of family and support can't be put into words — you have to live through it. I read a book about this, “Tribe”: the conditions around you can be terrible, but nothing can replace the feeling of brotherhood.

Remembering Vasia and Troia

Oleksandr: You have tattoos connected with the war. Tell us about them.

Artur: My whole right side is connected with the war. The highest one is about my little brother Vasia: “Forever a brother, forever an angel.” He's been gone for more than a year now… The words “Don't waste your life” are from when my sister-in-arms Troia, a 21-year-old combat medic, was killed. And words about my faith: “God is my shield, my rock, my hope.”

The pain lives inside. When Vasia was killed, I prayed for only one thing: “God, let me see him again, even for one minute. There's so much I didn't tell him…”

Vasia and I were as close as it gets. We slept next to each other in the barracks, separated only by a small partition. I promised his family I'd look after him. And in battle we were always side by side.

The last battle

Oleksandr: Tell us about your last battle.

Artur: We ended up completely surrounded. They lined us up and said: we need 16 volunteers who will go to the positions, but they'll come back from there either killed or wounded. I told my guys we weren't going. But Vasia looks at me and says: “Sonic, I'm going, I have to be there.” And he steps forward.

Everything turned upside down in my head. I already knew my wife was pregnant with our daughter. I was afraid of not coming home and not holding my child. But I see my brother is there. I walk and pray: “God, I'm afraid, but I have to be with him.” And the Lord seems to say: “Why do you doubt Me? I've led you all the way here — why do you think I won't take care of you now?”

I run up to the van and tell the commander: “I'm going, Vasia's there.” We went into the positions. Twelve hours without communication, the radios don't work, we're completely surrounded. Vasia recorded a video then: “They say true friends are known in hard times. Thank you, Sonic, for not leaving me.” And a 120mm mortar round lands. One brother-in-arms was killed instantly, a fragment tore Vasia's arm apart, I was thrown back. I come to, drag Vasia into the dugout, give him first aid — and realize half his arm is gone. I ask permission to pull out, and we broke through under heavy fire from every tree line. Guys are falling next to us…

I'm carrying Vasia on my shoulders and shouting Psalm 91: “A thousand may fall at your side, ten thousand at your right hand, but it will not come near you…” — “God, let us get out!” We made it to the evacuation point. In the vehicle we sang songs; I pressed my fingers into his wound so he wouldn't pass out. We got him to the hospital, and I called his mom: “We're wounded, Vasia's in serious condition, but he's alive.”

Unfortunately, later he couldn't be saved… His mom called to say thank you, and his grandmother at the cemetery asks: “Sonic, why didn't you save him this time?” And you don't know what to answer… I still write to him on Telegram when things are hard or when there's something to celebrate.

A third child and leaving the army

Oleksandr: How hard was it to make the decision to leave the army?

Artur: When I found out about the third pregnancy, I almost cried. My son recorded a video message for me: “Dad, you're our hero, but for the family you're the best. God has given us a new addition… You've done enough fighting!” It was very hard for me. We hadn't planned this pregnancy. And I promised my wife: if it's a boy — I stay for another rotation; if it's a girl — I leave the army. And ever since the first pregnancy I'd dreamed of a daughter, I knew her name — Emily, blue eyes, curly hair. And a daughter was born! A promise is a promise — you have to keep it.

Driving home, I cried so hard to the song “Home”… My mind understood that you're not going on leave, that you'll never again take up a weapon and go into a position shoulder to shoulder with the guys. For the first six months after leaving, my life was still in Telegram and Signal — I was monitoring everything happening at the positions. And then I learned how to live in the civilian world all over again.

Church, faith and supporting soldiers

Oleksandr: What was your relationship with the church like during your service and after you came back?

Artur: It's a painful topic. I missed church during my service. There was a period of disappointment in believers. In the “Christian bubble” everyone is so nice, but when you're on the razor's edge between life and death, you're often forgotten. Almost no one from church wrote to me the whole time. Sometimes you write: “Friends, we're surrounded, pray!” — and the next day no one even asks if you're alive. You don't need money — what matters is knowing you're remembered. And to write even a word from the front, sometimes I ran 300 meters to the Starlink under mortar fire.

My message to the church: don't cross people off when they're serving, and don't cross them off when they come back! Most suicides and alcoholism happen precisely when a soldier comes back and falls into a vacuum. If you want to celebrate a soldier's return — be with him and his family throughout his whole service!

Now we've moved to a different church. They have a ministry called “Our Soldier” — they came to see me at the front and in the hospital. Services and sermons there are in Ukrainian. The pastor tells the congregation: “The war is right outside the window — learn tactical medicine, build drones!” There's active faith there, not just words.

Oleksandr: And were you ever disappointed in God?

Artur: No, I wasn't disappointed in God. I was disappointed in people, because I placed too much hope in them. I have many questions for God about life and death: why do young people die, the best guys and girls, like Troia or Vasia, while others don't value life? But I know: if it weren't for God, I'd have stayed in that tree line. Everything I have — my home, my family, my life — is thanks to Him.

My message to the church: don't cross people off when they're serving, and don't cross them off when they come back!

Artur “Sonic” Safonov

“The war will never end for me”

Oleksandr: How did your adjustment to civilian life go?

Artur: Work helped me. I made a dream come true — I opened my own coffee shop, a fitness studio, a children's dance studio. I work as a massage therapist, a PE teacher at a Christian school and a boxing coach. I did the renovation with my own hands, day and night, to pull myself out of a depressive state. When I walked into the studio with a cup of coffee and saw the kids training, I understood: this is what I dreamed of. Life is easier for me when I have a lot to do and I'm being useful.

It was hard with my wife at first. Lera is wise; she'd say: “Artur, just go for a walk, listen to some music, drink some water.” We went to family retreats, talked a lot — and now we understand each other much better.

Oleksandr: Can you imagine the war ever ending for you?

Artur: No. The war will never end for me. It will end for those who watch it from afar. But for us it will go on forever — in the memory of fallen brothers-in-arms, in visiting their graves, in supporting their families. But recovery is possible. Our brotherhood doesn't end in the trenches — it lasts a lifetime. And I'm ready to support everyone who needs it.

Oleksandr: Thank you, Artur, for your service, your openness and your time!

Artur: Thank you!

The war will never end for me. It will end for those who watch it from afar.

Artur “Sonic” Safonov
