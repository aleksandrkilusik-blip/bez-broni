Season 1 Episode 4 · June 11, 2026

Denys Ivashchenko: “God can comfort the heart in a way no person can”

Hosts: Oleksandr Kyliushyk, Nadiia Kalachova

Denys is a veteran of the Armed Forces of Ukraine with the call sign “Chaplain,” an evangelical believer. Addiction in his youth, conversion, ministry to at-risk teenagers, mobilization, training in Britain, the “Skelia” regiment, and being wounded on his first combat mission. A conversation about the “little Nicodemuses” in the army, identity after amputation, reconciliation with his father, and the fear of death.

The conversation is in Ukrainian. Below is the full English text.

Introduction

Nadia: Welcome! You're listening to the podcast “Without Armor.” Today our guest is Denys Ivashchenko — a veteran whose call sign in the army was “Chaplain,” an evangelical believer. Denys, we're very glad you're with us today. We watched a few podcasts with you, and the way you talk is striking. In recent years — I think you'll agree — there have been plenty of trials. But you always say you're grateful, because God is with you in these trials. I want to ask: how did your journey with God begin? How did you come to know the God who now helps you go through all this?

From addiction to faith

Denys: My journey with God also began with difficult circumstances. As a teenager I started using drugs. I had a hard life without a father. And maybe those were exactly the best conditions for my heart to open up to God and receive Him. Through those circumstances God sent me a friend — my pastor, with whom we walked and still walk this path together.

It was very interesting, because I kept comparing everything with my old life, and the new life with God was truly vivid. Every day God amazed me with different miracles, circumstances in which He revealed Himself. When things were hard and I prayed, He simply answered. That's how I came to believe — really through miracles.

And in the same way He helped me leave the drug addiction. That was a great miracle. Because all people with addiction want to get rid of it, and from time to time they do — but they go back. And here I'm going through it with God and I don't want to go back — God gives me the ability not to go back. I stop feeling the desire for drugs. A very interesting and pleasant feeling: God was filling me with Himself, and everything I had wanted before was simply forgotten, drifted away, was no longer desirable.

A believer and weapons

Oleksandr: It's interesting that, already as a Christian, you held the view that a Christian shouldn't take up arms. What happened inside you when the full-scale war started and the draft notice came? What changed your attitude?

Denys: Great question. I'm glad you really listened to my interview. From the beginning of the war and throughout, I was changing — both spiritually and theologically. The war began, and I sort of understood that maybe my time would come to be there and defend. Although I still held the view that we should fight primarily without weapons.

But time passed, I was taken into a unit, I got to know the guys who were with me. And I understood: the enemy is very evil, and you have to defend yourself. You have to defend your friends, your family, and in general the people who support you and walk with you. I understood that there is also this path, and it's biblical too — even the people of Israel defended themselves when they were attacked. So I think I was ready. God prepared me.

I actually say that God was preparing me my whole life. I wanted to work with the brothers — somewhere in construction, with cars or something. But God kept closing those doors and opening up work for me with non-church people. So for me there was no division of “these go to church and those don't.” I was friends with everyone, I had a lot of friends outside church. And talking with soldiers wasn't hard for me at all.

“God is mobilizing me”

Oleksandr: And when you were mobilized, didn't you have thoughts like: “God, why? I'm called to something bigger. Why do I have to be there?”

Denys: I had all kinds of thoughts. But when the notice came, I was very anxious. I went into the forest, knelt down, prayed — and I understood: God was giving me the thought that it was He who was mobilizing me now for ministry. I understood that I had been preparing for this, and the time of my assignment had come.

Oleksandr: A new stage of ministry.

Denys: One hundred percent. That's how I felt it — and that's probably how it was. It was a hard military time, but a very good one spiritually. Because the best field for evangelism is there. The best time for discipleship is there. Because you're together 24/7. You simply live with the guys, go through hard circumstances together — like Jesus with His disciples. A really great time.

Britain and the “romance of war”

Nadia: In that conversation we listened to, you said you were in training in Britain and developed a real zeal for military service — you were good at it. And for some reason you called that a sin. But more important than why — what exactly about the army environment clicked for you?

Denys: The whole context of what was happening. We trained, sports, shooting, we were taught by reconnaissance — not that serious, a kind of light version. And I liked it. Everything was new, interesting, cool, captivating — the stuff we'd seen in movies.

Oleksandr: The romance of war.

Denys: Yes, there was a bit of romance. And I call it sin to some extent because I was forgetting why God had sent me there. And at times I got immersed in military stuff. Although God would bring me back and say: “That's not why you're here. You're here to be the church.”

Oleksandr: It's interesting that it awakened such zeal and interest in you. I was lazy — I just waited for it all to be over. And I wasn't in Britain, I can only envy you. See, thanks to the war you've been to the States and to Britain.

The call sign “Chaplain” and the “little Nicodemuses”

Nadia: Denys, how did your relationships with the guys develop? You didn't come up with the call sign “Chaplain” yourself. Maybe you had other options?

Denys: “Chaplain” was their idea — and God's through them, I believe. They didn't even give me a chance to choose another one. I had some options, but honestly I don't remember them anymore. I was just thinking about them, and the guys kept saying: “Don't even think about it. Here's your call sign — you'll be Chaplain, and that's it.” Sometimes they'd come up themselves and say: “Come on, tell us something about God.” So they themselves demanded it of me. That's cool.

Nadia: You said in that conversation that they'd sort of tease you and ask all kinds of questions. Which questions about God were hardest for you?

Denys: If I remember — for example, why war happens. Although actually it's not a hard question.

Nadia: Do you have an answer to it for yourself?

Denys: Of course, a simple one — the kind we're taught. The first answer: because all people are sinful, and we have a choice. War happens because everyone has their own truth. That's why there's misunderstanding in a community. But there is one Truth that brings us together — the Bible. If you live by it, there definitely won't be war.

Oleksandr: It's interesting what you said about discipleship and these conversations. Did it get to deeper conversations with the guys? Were there moments of repentance?

Denys: There wasn't that kind of direct repentance. There were lots of little Nicodemuses.

Oleksandr: Who came to test you?

Denys: No, no. They'd just come in the evening, sit down and want to talk, ask something. I could tell them something, answer questions. Quiet people like that. There were a lot of them. I just saw Bible stories coming alive next to me — it was really cool. There were also people who were adamant in their unbelief, but they still came and changed over time. I believe God, maybe through my example, brought them a little closer to faith. They saw in front of them a person who believes. And God really blessed me: when it was hard, I prayed, and He calmed me. Simple things, but people saw it. They understood that faith helps him.

The shepherd smells of sheep

Nadia: You compared brotherhood in the army with what you experienced in church. What is it between people at war that creates such a close bond?

Denys: Circumstances. Circumstances create one pain and one goal — that unites everyone. Just like in church: we have one vision, one mission, and that's why we're united in our ministry and actions. At war there's also one mission, one goal. And that goal is very big and important.

Nadia: Do you keep in touch now with the brothers-in-arms you were close to?

Denys: Yes. There aren't many — three guys — but from time to time we keep in touch.

Oleksandr: On the podcast we talk about “sterile Christianity” — the proper kind: doesn't drink, doesn't smoke, doesn't swear. And the army is a variety of people. Heavy, critical life circumstances lead to addictions coming back or appearing, you become harsh with words. Was it easy for you to accept soldiers and love them as they are?

Denys: I once heard a phrase from Yelysei Pronin: “The shepherd smells of sheep.” The shepherd has to be ready for all these circumstances. I was used to being among such people, nothing unusual. Everything happened automatically. I didn't even hear them swearing. Well, of course they swore, but first of all, they apologized for it. And then little by little almost everyone quit smoking — very few people in our unit smoked. And also my commander, who didn't swear himself, said: whoever swears will be punished. And added: “This is for you, Chaplain.” That was so nice.

Oleksandr: So back then you smelled of sheep. Very interesting how God surrounded you in this.

How to talk about God

Nadia: I have another question, Denys. About twelve years ago I heard a quote and wrote it down in my notebook. It said that the theologian Chesterton wrote that people talk about football, but really the only interesting thing to talk about is God. Were people interested in talking about God? How did you find a way to share the Gospel? I think everyone has their own strengths, and this came easily to you. What would you say to someone who's in the army now or getting ready to go: how do you win people over so there's that atmosphere where people, like Nicodemus, come in the evening and ask something?

Denys: We know many different methods of evangelism. For example, The Four bracelet — a really great thing that makes it very easy to share. And if I answer how to tell it — it's really great to know Bible stories. Something happens, you remember that the same thing happened in the Bible, and you can tell that story. The guys were really interested in just listening to an interesting story.

Oleksandr: Which story did you tell most often?

Denys: About Joseph.

Oleksandr: Which one? The one in Egypt?

Denys: The one in Egypt, yes.

Oleksandr: What made that story so captivating?

Denys: Joseph saved his people through his path. He went through slavery, prison, then became Pharaoh's right hand — and, being a Jew among Egyptians, saved the Jewish people. So any hard path has something interesting behind it. A very rich story from which you can pull anything and tell it in an interesting way. Universal.

And there's another way to talk about God — something I got from my pastor. He and my mom read the Bible this way, and I called it a “Bible horoscope.” Proverbs has a chapter for every day: you pick the date — for example, today is the seventh — you read chapter seven and find out what God wants in your life today. The guys were shocked: how can a horoscope be connected to the Bible? But we sat and read — and it's great. Proverbs is very simple and wise. To understand it you don't need Christian experience or education.

The “Skelia” regiment: the worst and the best time

Nadia: Denys, what happened after training? You come back from Britain — what did your service look like after that?

Denys: We came back and waited two days to see where we'd be taken. It was a very interesting period in my life. I'll tell you — I think it's the first time I'm sharing this in an interview. I understood that with my experience and physical condition I could be taken anywhere. But during training I understood: the best place is where people listen to you. Where they listen about God, where people have open hearts.

And I faced the question: where is it better to go? Where people will listen, but it'll be very hard? Because the two are connected. Or where it's okay, but they won't listen: comfortable conditions, safety, you won't be sent to the front line, to the zero line — you'll be, say, a driver. Or you'll be in hell itself, but there they'll listen and God will work. I prayed about it: “God, let it be as You want, as is more right.”

And God put me exactly where I needed to be. I was taken into the “Skelia” regiment. It was very, very hard psychologically, but not spiritually. Probably the worst and hardest time. There was pressure from some people on others, but there wasn't really pressure on me. I just accepted everything, God helped me go through those circumstances, and I also supported others. We already had a little community forming, a fellowship, we drew together. It was a great time. I'm glad God sent me into such a hard environment, where He worked through me and encouraged me by it. It was hard, but very gratifying. I remember it as a good period in my life — even though probably the worst.

Nadia: I was just thinking: which period of his life will Denys call bad? Because back there — addiction, but God was preparing your heart. Here — hell, but an environment where God was working.

Denys: That was probably the worst moment in my life. But it was good.

The year he prayed for

Nadia: Did you have a desert period, when it was hard?

Denys: Before I was mobilized. I even remember the prayer: “God, give me a year to rest, to reboot spiritually, so I can serve and find my calling.” And now almost a year has passed since mobilization — exactly the year I prayed for has just ended.

Oleksandr: And did you manage to rest?

Denys: I don't know! That's how it goes with God sometimes, it's very interesting.

Nadia: What was that desert period before mobilization about?

Denys: The last period was just hard, because everything was monotonous. We served in church, did a lot of work with youth. Our church works with at-risk teenagers who, like I once did, have drug addiction. All of that was really relevant. But I didn't feel a personal touch in the ministry, in my calling. And it was draining me.

When I was mobilized, I didn't even think my path was changing. I planned to work with youth, because I feel their pain — I went down that path myself. But God led me another way. So there was some dissatisfaction: I'm supposed to keep witnessing to youth, helping.

Oleksandr: You asked for a year of rest — and then you get taken into the army.

Denys: Incredible, right. There was some dissatisfaction with God, some questions. But whenever a question came up, it was immediately covered by a good answer. It wasn't like I stewed in it.

For example, about the youth. When I was taken to the enlistment office, the chief — a non-believer — says: “If you serve youth, then go,” — back then I was being offered to build some fortifications, — “and you'll come back to your teenagers as someone who's been there. You'll have something to tell them.” At first I was a bit angry with him: what nonsense is this? But over time I understood that God was speaking to me through him. Because now I really can — these teenagers' parents are fighting. Teenagers are going through the hardest time now, because it all falls on the hardest period, adolescence: the war, their parents, and everything else. So the teen ministry didn't go anywhere. It stayed, it's just different now.

Holding a hand and talking about Jesus

Nadia: You told how you held the hand of a soldier you didn't know and talked to him about Jesus' love.

Denys: That's true.

Oleksandr: How? You're lying there bandaged — I guess the adrenaline was still doing its job?

Denys: The tourniquets were on, everything's squeezing. But when you go to a position, you've already said goodbye to life, because you understand you might not come back. And in fact you're already a fearless person — because you're already there. I thought: if I die, at least I'll take someone with me by the hand. It's a very interesting place in general: you no longer live an earthly life, because it can end at any moment, and you understand that. You live as if heavenly life might start right now. And so your thoughts are pure. You're not thinking about how to pay the electricity bill — you're thinking about how to bring one more person to God. That's cool.

Nadia: Cool. We should have that feeling here: live one day at a time, dream of heaven and live every moment of life with God — every conversation, every glance, every coffee with someone. That would be great.

Oleksandr: I also used to say I was ready to die and go to God. But I remember tank fire following us, and I was running — running so well! And I say: “God, remember how I said I was ready to die? Let's not do it now. Let me make it there after all. Let's do it next time.” I wouldn't call that heroism. When you say you're ready to go to God, it still seems to me there's some fear, some human pull to hold on. Was there a moment when you were afraid and wanted to hold on to life?

Denys: It was more where I saw a response: I'd speak a word — and people responded. I understood that God was using me as an instrument in His hands, and I liked it. That's why I wanted to hold on to life — I wanted to hold people's hands and tell them. And also after being wounded, when I talked with guys after amputations. I want to live when I understand that my tree and its fruit are needed by someone. These people talk with me — and they see God. I'm like a conduit. That's very gratifying and gives me the desire to hold on to life, to grow and to plan something.

Oleksandr: Otherwise, in principle, you could go to heaven too. As Paul said: it's better for me to depart, but for your sake I'm still here. But don't you sometimes fail to understand God? You serve brilliantly, you tell people about God a lot, and He nearly takes you when you're on the edge of life and death. No feeling of injustice, no complaints against God?

A new identity

Denys: Not really. I know I'm not a very good person. I understand myself, I understand my sins. And it's great grace and God's mercy that He uses me, that He does something through me. So thank God I'm still alive, can be here, and He's still doing something through me. And my merit in this is smaller than a mustard seed.

It's just a question of our identity: what we associate ourselves with, what our plan for life is. People who don't know God compare themselves, for example, with magazine covers: “I want to look like that — the figure, the house, the car.” Their identity is in that, like the American dream: everyone dreams of achieving it. But believers have their own identity — it's Jesus and His life. So there's nothing strange here. We follow the path of Christ, and one of the things that awaits us is suffering.

And the other thing — well, if I don't have legs, so what? I can still go on living. I'll just live without legs. There's nothing special about it. Many people live like that from birth — and they live. You can live. So I adjusted quickly. The phantom pains completely went away in just four months. The phantom sensations remained, but the pain went. Because I'm sure: I convinced myself and understood that this is who I am, I'll keep living like this, and in this I'm whole. My soul, my spirit accepts these circumstances — and moves on, lives. God gave me His identity.

Because I'm sure: I convinced myself and understood that this is who I am, I'll keep living like this, and in this I'm whole.

The first combat mission

Nadia: You were wounded on your first combat mission?

Denys: The first and only one.

Nadia: Didn't you feel: why so fast?

Denys: Honestly, I did. But knowing my zeal, I think: thank God it happened like that. Because I still felt like a soldier from time to time. And after that mission I definitely would have felt like a hell of a soldier — because the mission was very interesting.

Oleksandr: And what does “an interesting combat mission” mean?

Denys: We had to get into the rear and break the line — assault it, so our guys could come in, take positions and push the enemy out of the forest. I was going first in the group; we had to cross a field. It turned out I didn't have a radio — one for two people. I took off first. And the rest were told over the radio to go back and put on anti-drone cloaks. They went back — and I went alone. It all happened kind of absurdly, but I believe that's how God planned it and how it had to be.

I got there — and met two Russians. Sasha, have you heard this story?

Nadia: No, we haven't.

Denys: Maybe I told it once somewhere and you missed it. I just try not to tell it in front of believers.

Oleksandr: You can tell it in front of us. We're “Without Armor” — you have to tell it.

Denys: Because believers react differently to circumstances like that. But I'll tell you how I see it. I was without my glasses and couldn't see anything — everyone looks the same. I say: “Guys, come here!” I thought my guys had overtaken me and come out from the other side.

Nadia: In Ukrainian?

Denys: In Ukrainian. Because of the distance they apparently didn't understand anything. And I was in Ukrainian uniform, with the flag and everything. They came up, and I realized they were Russians. But without weapons — we were in their rear, and they didn't expect to meet us.

Oleksandr: So you just called — and they came over. Okay.

Denys: That's only the beginning. I had my rifle. And then they took it from me. It was my first mission, I'm a believer — it didn't even cross my mind to shoot people, I wasn't ready for that. I pretended to be one of their recon guys. They started checking me over the radio — it took a long time, about ten minutes. And they took my rifle. I have no idea how I gave it up. An ordinary soldier hearing this would say: “What nonsense is this? Who even are you?”

There were two of them. One loaded my rifle and pointed it at me. That's it — I'd already started praying. The other was on the radio waiting for me to be checked. They were apparently nervous: killing your own recon guy is probably not great. Time passed. I was already thinking: “God, maybe one more second — and I'll be with You.” Very strange feelings.

A few more minutes passed — and then my guys come in right behind me and shout: “Palianytsia! Glory to Ukraine!” They fell for it too — it was their first mission as well. Non-believers. And I'm standing there without a rifle, a loaded weapon pointed at me, and that guy says: “Well, let's finish this.” And in that moment everything happens: I just grab a rifle from one of my guys.

Oleksandr: Intense. I can't imagine.

Denys: I grab it: “Listen, I've changed my mind. Wait.” I manage to realize the safety is on, I flick it off. And the one aiming at me was already ready to shoot.

Oleksandr: Wait, so he loaded it and put it on safety?

Denys: A round was chambered, but the rifle was on safety. They went into the assault with safeties on because the grass was tall — they were afraid of firing by accident. In short, I yank the rifle away and neutralize those Russians who were against me. That's it. My guys are in shock: I grabbed a weapon from them.

Nadia: From your own guys?

Denys: From my own, because mine had been taken. But I'm glad I was truly defending myself. Because when I called them over, they were unarmed — they could simply have been taken prisoner. I didn't think of that — and I don't think anyone would have let me anyway. I'm very fair: I won't shoot unarmed people. “I'll take one from my guys now — come on, so it's fair.” But I believe it was God's plan. And how He did it is simply amazing.

Nadia: And you were wounded that same day?

Denys: No, only three days later. Next, another guy and I go to assault the line — and there was no one there anymore. Lots of dugouts, but empty. Those two somehow wandered in there by some miracle — they were just unlucky. I say God immersed me in it in “light mode.” I saw everything, experienced everything myself. And now I understand soldiers. I lost brothers-in-arms when we were assaulting: the enemy came in behind us, and my brothers-in-arms were killed. God led me down this path so that I would understand many people.

We went down that path. I still went first, but now with a radio — I took it from the Russians. The commanders are in shock: “We're totally shocked at how calm you are.” And I tell them: “Yes, commander, I'm taking charge of the group, I have the radio.” Later they say: “We just watched all that action from the drone — and he's calmly talking.”

Oleksandr: I can't imagine what those watching it felt. I think they'd already buried you.

Denys: No, everyone knew I was a believer. Everyone knew me there — that kind of reputation spread about me, I don't know how.

Oleksandr: If you'd kept serving, they'd probably always send you first. “Go sort it out. You don't need a rifle.”

Pastoral ministry at war

Nadia: There's a book — “Light in the Valley of the Shadow of Death,” stories of believers about how they're living through the war. When I read it, I noticed: one person goes to war and sees himself there as an evangelist — for example, Zhenia Yazvinskyi, Sasha's friend, describes his story that way. And you read another story and realize that this believer's mission is to be a soldier, to command. So what was your ministry at war, after all?

Denys: I can't say it was just one thing. Probably more pastoral. I wasn't a commander, but a lot of people listened to me. I wasn't an evangelist — it's just that my life: the way I lived, prayed, read the Bible — was an example for other guys. And they just asked. In fact I didn't even feel I was carrying out some ministry. I just lived among the guys and openly showed that I'm a believer.

Oleksandr: Are you planning to get prosthetics?

Denys: I'm already on prostheses.

Oleksandr: What was your first dream once you were back on your own feet? I also got my prosthetics at Superhumans, and I know the dreams can be even absurd — like going to the bathroom on your own feet.

Denys: It was a long-held dream: to be at a service in my church. I've lost count of how many times I've been at a service on prostheses. I even stood with the worship team on prostheses.

Nadia: Awesome! And you sing — you mentioned that at the beginning. We have a question about music in the lightning round, so we guessed right.

Dad

Nadia: One more thing that's striking in your interview. When you told how you came to God, you said: “I grew up without a father.” And precisely after your service and injury, your relationship with your dad got better. Tell us, please, how that happened. How is it possible after so many years?

Denys: That's another thing that encourages me on this path — a kind of bonus from God. When I was taken, my dad started actively helping: they gathered things for me, looked for gear. And after I was wounded he came and said: “I want life to be different, not like before.” Before that we barely talked. And now — it's really great. Probably what every son dreams of.

I don't know how it happened. For example, when I was taken into the army, my sister — my dad's daughter — started coming to my church. Because our church building burned down, and we moved to hold services in the city, in a rented space where we held youth meetings. So one thing after another, like a chain, it had an effect.

Nadia: Didn't you ask your dad why he wasn't involved in your life before, and now — helping a son who's serving? What played such a role? And was it easy for you? Because usually there's resentment toward a dad who wasn't there your whole life.

Denys: There's no resentment. When I came to faith, the resentment went away. Before conversion it was there, and then there was a kind of rethinking. I understood that the Bible says: “Honor your father and mother.” And I understood that different circumstances happen in life, especially for people outside the church. Why be offended at people who don't know God, weren't in church — well, they split up. For the world these are normal things: divorce, life without a father.

And I began to look at the situation differently. I began to keep the commandment to honor my father: to call more often — just for the sake of keeping the commandment. I was curious. I always understood: if God wants us to do this, it means there's something in it, it'll affect something. A small path: I'd call once every six months, just ask how he was doing. I felt a kind of kairos — that I needed to call. And I called.

I could share this with my teenagers — they also had problems with their parents. And I showed by example: you need to talk to your parents, whatever the circumstances. And then it poured out into blessing. I changed from the inside — and then God changed the circumstances.

If I pull a little life hack out of my life: when something hard, wrong, unfair happens to you, something you didn't plan — think about how to behave so that in the end God's name is glorified. Circumstances will change. But your reaction in them should bring some result — otherwise what's the point of everything that's happening? Hard circumstances will pass and may leave only a bad imprint in memory. Or they can become God's glory, something incredible, a good story, an example.

The fear of death

Nadia: You have a Telegram channel. I went in and looked at what you post. You posted a video where a pastor in America answers, in Russian, a soldier's question: “What do I do with the fear of death?” That man is wounded, but he has to go back to the war. And I thought: interesting, why does Denys post this American pastor's answer instead of writing his own? What would you say to the guys who may be listening to this conversation, who are in the army and have a fear of death? Some wanted to go, some didn't, but now they're there.

Denys: I don't remember anymore what that pastor said. So I'll say what left an imprint on me. Yesterday I read in the Bible: “Present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and pleasing to God.” That means our actions, our deeds affect something. Wherever we are, whatever we go through, even at death's door — our life will still affect something. And probably we should worry less about death and more about what good I'm bringing right now.

And even if I die — how will that affect the world? Soldiers are making a great sacrifice now. They give their lives, their health, their time, their future. Because when a soldier comes back, he can no longer live as before. He's left with memories, the emotions he went through, flashbacks, sleepless nights. That's a lot. It's truly a great sacrifice. And it pours directly onto Ukrainians — it's a great defense of the country. It's incredibly important. It's a purpose for which it's a great honor to become that sacrifice, to be part of such a great process. The Bible says: “Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends.” And the fear of death… I don't know how to answer such hard questions. It's very hard.

Oleksandr: But I believe we're left with the opportunity to help others through the lens of our experience. And, as Nadia said, not to hold up as examples those who are abroad — I'm not against those abroad — but to use the experience we have and that God gave us, and to help and influence people. So I really hope you'll shape your thoughts and write in your channel about what you've been through. It's truly valuable. I think shyness is out of place here, because it will only limit the experience God gave you. And someday maybe we'll read your book.

Nadia: Otherwise, later Americans will be reposting you. Our first guest, Roman Pikulik… We have leadership courses, foreign teachers come. I thought: a soldier, a major — he could teach a good leadership course. I floated the idea — and Roman declined. Lightning round

Nadia: The lightning round is my favorite. Just a short answer. You went from drugs to ministry in the church. What one word would you say to someone who's searching for a way out of addiction right now?

Denys: The first thing that came to mind — delight. We delighted in drugs, got high, loved them. And to change, you need to delight in something else — in God. That is, take off your old clothes and put on new ones.

Nadia: What song or music was with you in your recovery?

Denys: A lot. In the army there was the song “Jesus Is My Best Friend.”

Nadia: And when you were in the hospital, in that hard period, what did you listen to?

Denys: I forgot what it's called, but it had the words “I will go… to the distant mountains.” And I felt those words — “I will go.” I felt God was giving me hope: I'll walk, I'll go. When you're in pain, you catch every moment.

Nadia: What quality in people have you come to value most after the war, after the experience of brotherhood-in-arms?

Denys: Honesty and simplicity. Transparency. That's important. Say we've completed an assault and we go into a trench with friendly forces — they were waiting for us. We sit down, but we still don't know whether they're ours or not. We sit, start talking a little. Some are from Dnipro — Russian slips into their speech. Creepy. You're on adrenaline, you've been in combat for a day, you don't trust anyone. And they've got a Russian rifle standing there too. So honesty and transparency — that's already good.

Nadia: What would you say to yourself — to the Denys who was there before the war, with your whole life background?

Denys: First thought: “You must learn to love suffering.” Today I read in the Epistle of James, chapter one: when trials come to you, receive them with joy. Because trials produce patience. That's truly beneficial, it changes us. It changes me, my brain, my heart, my relationship with God. So it's a good tool.

Nadia: And the last question. You mentioned that at war everything is somehow more alive. What does being alive mean to you now, after everything you've been through?

Denys: There's that thing about rose-colored glasses. We put them on and live the way we want to see this world — through our own worldview: everyone should love me and so on. But when we put on biblical glasses, we see what's really happening. That other people are in pain and need help. That a lot is happening that we don't want, but it has to be seen. And that is aliveness: you're here, in this place, in authenticity, in reality. Being alive means seeing what's happening and understanding that God wants to change something here through you and help these people.
