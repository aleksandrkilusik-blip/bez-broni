Season 1 Episode 6 · August 21, 2026

Mykhailo Todikash: “I don't know how to evangelize. I serve practically”

Hosts: Oleksandr Kyliushyk, Nadiia Kalachova

Mykhailo is a military anesthesiologist who served in the army from 2014 to 2025. He now works at a military hospital in Mukachevo and, through his church, serves wounded soldiers and veterans. A conversation about war through a doctor's eyes, the line between professional detachment and becoming hardened, evangelism by personal example, the “Living Fires” gatherings for veterans, and the “arm's reach rule.”

The conversation is in Ukrainian. Below is the full English text.

Introduction

Oleksandr: Today we're glad to welcome our guest Mykhailo Todikash. Mykhailo was a military anesthesiologist from 2014 to 2025. He still works as a doctor in a military hospital and continues to serve wounded soldiers and veterans — that's now part of your church ministry. Is that all right, Mykhailo?

Mykhailo: Yes, hello. You said everything correctly.

Oleksandr: Thank you so much, and we're very glad you came from Mukachevo for this conversation. Nadia and I were thinking about how to start this podcast and remembered our earlier conversation. When we asked what might be triggering, you said that the question “how are you?” isn't the best way to start a conversation. So… how are you?

Mykhailo: Great! The weather's nice in Mukachevo, a bit of rain in Lviv. Everything's good, thank God.

Eleven years of military medicine

Oleksandr: Mykhailo, for eleven years you were specifically a military doctor — you saw a lot, you saw the war. It's a long period, and when it started you were still a young man. How did this experience affect your character, your values, the kind of person you are?

Mykhailo: I think it affected me one hundred percent and left its mark. I think all these eleven years I was useful. From medical university and all these years I worked in the army and helped the wounded and the sick — both in quiet, calm places and in eastern Ukraine. For me it definitely left a mark. And I think I brought some measure of benefit to our country.

Oleksandr: And which character traits sharpened, became more pronounced during this time? If for these eleven years you'd been an ordinary anesthesiologist in an ordinary hospital — but this experience was specific. How did it affect your character in particular?

Mykhailo: The role of anesthesiologist — both in civilian life and in military medicine — strongly affects both the patient and the doctor, it leaves a mark. Being under that kind of stress all these years… it certainly didn't improve my health. But on the other hand, that's what I was trained for so many years at medical school. So I think it was worth it.

War through a doctor's eyes

Oleksandr: I'm curious what it means to be a military doctor. What did your service look like, and what kind of war did you see through a doctor's eyes? Because so far we've had people who served in the infantry, in tank units, and everyone has their own lens.

Mykhailo: For me, war in terms of military medicine is working with dirty wounds. Wounds with clothing and dirt in them, and we work with all of it.

Oleksandr: So you were providing not first aid but physician-level care — and the wounded still come to you in that state? I thought they'd already be prepared somewhat at the first-aid stage.

Mykhailo: If there are more than two wounded, it's impossible to prepare them in a short time the way the textbook says. In reality the situation is completely different.

Oleksandr: When you're working with the wounded, do you feel that it's war? Or are you just treating a patient? Does it stir up anger toward the enemy? What do you feel in that moment?

Mykhailo: Of course you feel that it's war. These injuries differ critically from chronic illnesses or any oncology — there's no comparison. Gunshot wounds are usually received by young people, and it's even harder to work when you understand: in front of you isn't a patient who's been ill for years, but a fresh wound that takes life very quickly. If you don't stop the bleeding or restore the airway, a 22-year-old guy can die in three minutes. All of this is radically different from civilian medicine.

Burnout and the line of hardening

Oleksandr: But that doesn't stop you? Over eleven years there's far too much of all this. It's hard to show empathy when you see such a stream of people.

Mykhailo: According to European colleagues, an anesthesiologist burns out after eight years.

Oleksandr: Wow.

Mykhailo: And according to Mykhailo… I know my colleagues, all my senior colleagues: they work understanding that it's their duty, that's what they were trained for. Duty to the country also plays a role, I think. They're professionals and they have to do it. And I know many colleagues who voluntarily extended their rotation in the east. That's normal in medicine: someone doesn't want to go, and there are doctors who stay there for a long period and work.

Oleksandr: And how are you with burnout? How did you cope with it?

Mykhailo: You know, you just work, that's it. With the wounded you don't really have contact. You only ask whether it hurts and where it hurts. You don't even ask their name — sometimes there's no time, and sometimes the wounded are brought in already unconscious. So you just do your job and carry out your duties. For me it's simply a flow.

Oleksandr: I remember when I was brought to the stabilization point. They're stitching me up, and the medic says: “Do you mind if I put on some music? It's boring otherwise.” And I'm thinking: how can that be — there's an open leg in front of you and you're putting on music? So where's the line between professional detachment, when you do your job in a measured way, and having become hardened? Because it seems to me that when you see so many wounded, you harden.

Mykhailo: Yes, I agree with that too. I think I've become angrier. It affected me: I've become less sensitive, maybe somewhat stricter — it shows both with my kids at home and at work. When everything's calm, it doesn't really show. But in a stressful situation, or when I see that something is being done unfairly or wrongly, I react immediately. And it seems to me it gets stronger every year.

Oleksandr: How long is a shift?

Mykhailo: 24 hours.

Oleksandr: And you work non-stop?

Mykhailo: Non-stop. You get a call at eight in the morning: “I need you to relieve me in the operating room.” You take over — and then you're the one calling at eight the next morning that you need to be relieved. Sometimes, while a patient is being prepped for surgery, there are five or ten minutes — then you manage to grab a bite, have a coffee.

Who to talk this experience through with

Nadia: I wanted to highlight another point. Sasha, remember when we were preparing for the conversation, I asked… Mykhailo, do you have people with whom you can talk through this experience? Sasha, say, as a veteran can talk through his experience with other veterans, including Christians. But a doctor's experience differs from a soldier's. Are there any medical — Christian or simply professional — circles where you can talk this through and where you understand each other?

Mykhailo: That's the hospital where I work. All my colleagues go where I went. Constantly at meetings or when we see each other we recall our rotations, tell about cases, share experience: who did what in this or that situation. Because the cases were very different — different injuries, different emotional moments.

Nadia: But as doctors you share more about medical things. What about personal ones? I don't know whether emotions and feelings fade into the background. You didn't save a person — and that's hard to live through. People who work, for example, in social work have supervisors: you come once a month and talk about your professional challenges. Is there anything like that for doctors?

Mykhailo: No, we don't have that. I think with such a flow of wounded everyone understands each other without words. Everyone knows what to do, because the war has been going on for many years. And if something special happens, it's discussed in narrow circles, and that's it. And veterans who aren't from medicine probably wouldn't be interested in hearing it.

Nadia: I'm scared to listen to it.

Oleksandr: And when you were there and called your wife — what did you talk about?

Mykhailo: We all know what to tell our wives: everything's fine here, everything's calm, I've had breakfast, I'm wearing my hat. I know my wife always prays for me and worries. I think it would affect her negatively if I told her what I'd done in the last 24 hours. There's no point — just so as not to do harm.

Oleksandr: And were you able to switch from what you'd seen to talking about civilian life — about the kids, about what's happening in your wife's life?

Mykhailo: I think partly. I can partly understand her worries, and she mine. To some percentage.

A doctor first, then a soldier

Nadia: Mykhailo, another important question. You were in the army for eleven years. What did it mean for you to be both a military doctor and a Christian? How did that show in your service?

Mykhailo: We were always taught: you yourselves say that the word “military” comes first, and then “doctor.” Although I always said that I'm a doctor first, and then a soldier. In the army you understand that orders vary — medical or not, it doesn't matter — but they still have to be carried out. But a doctor is a doctor. To help the patient and do no harm — that's the main thing for a doctor. That, I think, is the difference.

Oleksandr: And as a Christian? Roma — you know him — told us that on his first rotation in 2014 he came with literature. To read himself, not to hand out — he clarified that, because I asked again. And how did you, as a Christian, come in?

Mykhailo: I think the best and most effective evangelism is the personal, practical example of how you live. When we were stationed in Avdiivka, at first everyone thought I was sick. Not only was I from western Ukraine, I also didn't drink or smoke — that was something completely incomprehensible. But over time it passes very quickly when you show that at any moment you remain faithful to God. Holiday or not — you still behave rightly.

By the way, in Avdiivka I went to church every Sunday. There was a very good church there and a good pastor, Roman. I took the military ambulance and drove to the service. The first time I came, they got really scared — the pastor came up right away: “Who are you here for?” And afterward, after the service, we drove all the grannies home. At first the neighbors were scared, and then the grannies were already waiting for me to come.

Oleksandr: The grannies know how to ride in ambulances.

Mykhailo: You know, I'm the kind of person who isn't very good at evangelizing. I'm more of a practitioner. And I believe a one-off event doesn't work. You need to work for a long time and simply live rightly. A person living next to you sees that you lead a Christian way of life — and that will be greater evangelism than if you push magazines or something like that on them. I think the period when stadiums were filled has passed in Christianity. Now individual conversation and personal example work more. People no longer believe in loud speeches. They analyze Christians — and that's how they check whether God is really there.

Oleksandr: And over eleven years, did people come up with personal questions?

Mykhailo: Of course. Even at veterans' gatherings, when we sit and talk, people ask: “What church do you go to? Which God do you believe in and serve?” And then the word is sown. It's a completely different conversation when the initiative comes from someone who's interested.

Oleksandr: You serve veterans specifically in your church. But in churches people often say: “I don't know how to approach a soldier, what to say so as not to offend and not make things worse.” And because of that they distance themselves even more. What would you say to these people?

Mykhailo: I'd say: just work at your own level, to the max. If there's a chance to talk — talk. If there's a chance to help — help. If you can share something from your own experience or about someone — do it. I think it's important both for that person and for you. And it will become a foundation for something bigger.

I think the best and most effective evangelism is the personal, practical example of how you live.

Mykhailo Todikash

“Why do you care more than everyone else?”

Nadia: Mykhailo, Sasha touched on ministry to soldiers, and I want to go deeper. A thought is forming in my head: you've served — we keep saying the number, eleven years — you've made your contribution to the country and the war. You could allow yourself to step back: wife, kids, a completely different ministry. Why do you keep serving soldiers, and how exactly? There's even a cheekier question: “Why do you care more than everyone else?”

Mykhailo: I think it's my duty as a Christian living in a time of war. Sitting at home and saying “I've done my service” while the war goes on is wrong. If you can be useful — be useful to the end. You can't prove, first of all to yourself, that the war is over for you. Even when the war ends, there will still be veterans, and they'll need to be worked with and understood. Someone definitely has to, because the syndrome the guys have isn't going anywhere.

“Living Fires”

Mykhailo: And what is my ministry? We organize gatherings for veterans called “Living Fires.” It can be fishing, art therapy at the church, some outings. We held one of these gatherings at the hospital too. It's really great when veterans can talk with someone. We have a part called the “reflection circle”: we light a campfire, everyone sits in a circle, and we pose questions. For example: “How do you communicate with civilians when you come back from the war?” And people share — people want to tell something. We ask: “Do you need these gatherings? Are we doing this right?” And the pastor who's with us asks: “How can the church be useful to veterans?” That's how we get feedback, and we try to apply it right away.

I'll tell one example. It was fishing at a lake in the mountains not far from Mukachevo. We brought wounded soldiers from the hospital, some of them in wheelchairs. One wounded man hadn't left the medical facility for eight months. And another was a veteran around 50–55. He quietly fished the entire gathering, didn't want to talk to anyone — just sat with his fishing rod, and that's it. We didn't understand why he was behaving like that. But when we gathered the circle and asked the question, he simply stood up and shared: a week earlier he had returned from captivity. He'd been a prisoner for three years. And all that time he lived with the thought that someday he'd go fishing. There was such a great atmosphere there — he lived for this gathering.

So, thank God, we helped this man adapt psychologically, even if just for a moment. And the other wounded man said that a gathering like this replaces a year with a psychologist or in treatment — he got so many positive emotions. I think it also encourages church members. We record everything on video, edit it and show the church that this is exactly how we work with veterans.

A challenge for a pastor

Oleksandr: I think there was another example, about a challenge for a pastor in talking with veterans. I'd like you to mention it too, because it's very motivating. You don't only serve yourselves, you also challenge other ministers.

Mykhailo: Definitely. I'd like churches to get involved. I know the Irpin church is developing this a lot — we even look at them and take things for ourselves. They organize really great gatherings at their church and bring together a lot of veterans. And I truly would like our churches to hold such gatherings more actively in any format and to serve all categories of people. Because during war there aren't only veterans. There are veterans' wives, families, children. There are widows of the fallen. There are women whose husbands are in captivity — they also need to be worked with and cared for. There are displaced people who haven't gone anywhere. There are lots of options.

Nadia: But “I don't know how to approach or how to start a conversation” is still heard. Can you tell us your example — how you showed how to approach?

Mykhailo: We invited the church pastor to one of these gatherings. Again it was fishing — about twenty people were fishing. In general we gather up to 50–60 veterans, their families and kids for these events. There's a separate program for the kids: our sisters get involved, there are all kinds of interactive activities, coloring books, toy fish and fishing rods — kids love that.

And I asked the pastor to go up to all these veterans — maybe someone would want to talk with him. When he'd gone around everyone, I went up and asked: “Did anyone shut down? Did anyone not want to talk?” And he was very surprised: one hundred percent of them got to know him and talked with him, no one sent him away. I think that's a really great example. That's the statistic: it's exactly at gatherings like these, when the church takes the first step, that you can talk with the guys. Even just a conversation can change someone's mood, help them forget for a while — and that's already good. That's how God is glorified, and that's how you can see the church exists, that it's alive.

We've also organized accommodation for relatives of wounded soldiers at our church. If a veteran is being treated at the Mukachevo hospital, his loved ones — wife, mom — can stay at the church as long as needed, for the whole period of treatment. We have a separate building with rooms, and we host them for free. If they want — we invite them to services or to talk with the pastor. And people do reach out and come. I think that's also a good way of making contact with soldiers.

Why some churches serve and others don't

Nadia: Mykhailo, I'm curious: does your church know how to serve and see the need because people from it are serving, and thanks to your experience as a military doctor? Because it seems that your church does a lot, while others don't start this kind of ministry — they simply don't see it and don't know how to begin.

Mykhailo: I think at some level all churches do something for soldiers. I can't say that no one does anything. We do it because we have the opportunity. We have brothers, church members, who are serving now. We show each of them on the screen and pray for them, we remember them in prayer every Sunday. And if they have some need — we try to respond. We also have the financial capacity. Maybe other churches don't, because this takes money too.

I think the priority right now is veterans and soldiers. Of course there are other needs, and I'm not saying we shouldn't care for low-income families or other categories of people — we absolutely should. But we live in a time of war, and everyone needs to understand this — not only soldiers, but civilians too. So that later, when their children or grandchildren ask what they did, they can give the right answer.

The arm's reach rule

Nadia: You call it the arm's reach rule, right?

Mykhailo: Yes, I heard the phrase somewhere and I live by it. The arm's reach rule means: do what you can touch. That is, according to your ability: if you can help in some way — help, instead of complaining that the state is bad or someone else is bad. Everything that depends on you and that you can reach — reach out and do it.

Nadia: And do many people around you live by a similar principle? Or is it only you who cares?

Mykhailo: This thought came up recently. We had a regional leaders' conference at the church, and one pastor said: when you carry a ministry, there comes a moment when you ask yourself: “Am I the only one who cares about this?” And the right answer is — yes, you're the only one who cares. Because everyone is busy with their own work and takes care of it. And your work — for me, for example, ministry to veterans — only you care about it, because you're responsible for it.

So when you see that you're not supported somewhere, that people don't want to join the team, don't want to get involved — you need to understand that this is yours to care about. When you understand that, it becomes easier and calmer. You understand that everyone is busy with other ministries and taking care of their own. And you respond accordingly: you try to cover all the needs yourself as much as possible.

Everything that depends on you and that you can reach — reach out and do it.

Mykhailo Todikash

What gives strength

Nadia: After everything you've been through, what gives you strength and hope at this stage of life?

Mykhailo: What gives me strength, one hundred percent, is that I can be useful — it doesn't matter whether in medicine or in the army. When I understand that I have to be useful in my place, that gives strength. And when I see support: there's some good work, and people support it because they clearly understand what it's for — not somewhere far away, but right next to them. Then it gives strength, and you want to grow in it, to extend that arm's reach. That's what gives strength, inspiration and the desire to be useful. I think that's the most important thing.

What to tell the grandchildren

Oleksandr: I was at your house, you showed me your plans — what you want to build, all kinds of little bridges. And many years from now your grandchildren will come to you and ask about this war. What would you like to tell them?

Mykhailo: I think I'd tell them, without any reproach, that I helped people. And I'd give them advice: God forbid they should ever live in a time of war — but if they do, to be courageous and useful.

I think this question is more relevant for civilians than for soldiers. If you're a civilian, you have more questions about what to do. In the army there are clear goals and tasks: you work, and that's it. I'd answer like this. If you were a soldier — try your hardest to do everything so the war ends, and don't lose a Christian approach in war: be useful, show love to your neighbor, do the right things. And if you're a civilian — be useful to others: to displaced people, to those who need your help specifically. Not someone else, not the state, not the mayor — you. Whoever you can help — help.

That's what everything is made of. It's the first puzzle piece — in the church, in the country, in medicine, in any team. What you can do — do it. And that's where everything will begin.
